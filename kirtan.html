Themes · Kīrtan and the holy name

The name that carries the One it names

Every tradition that loves God also loves to call on God's name. In the bhakti tradition, singing the holy name together, kīrtan, is more than a practice among many. It is the path that the scriptures single out for our age.

nāmnām akāri bahudhā nija-sarva-śaktis / tatrārpitā niyamitaḥ smaraṇe na kālaḥ “You have given Yourself many names, and placed all Your power in each of them. You have set no hard rules for remembering them.” Śrī Caitanya Mahāprabhu, Śikṣāṣṭakam 2 (our rendering)

Nāma and nāmī

Why a name can hold God

An ordinary name only points to something. Saying “water” does not quench thirst. The Vedic teaching is that the Divine is different. Because God is absolute, God's name is not separate from God. Calling the name is meeting the One who is named.

nāma cintāmaṇiḥ kṛṣṇaś caitanya-rasa-vigrahaḥ / pūrṇaḥ śuddho nitya-mukto ’bhinnatvān nāma-nāminoḥ “The holy name is a wish-fulfilling jewel. It is [the all-attractive Personality of God] Himself, full of consciousness and sweetness: complete, pure and ever free, because the name and the named are one.” Padma Purāṇa, quoted in Caitanya-caritāmṛta, Madhya-līlā 17.133 (our rendering)

This is why, on the fourth level of awareness, the Divine is known through nāma, the name, together with form, qualities and activities. The name is the doorway that is open to everyone.

Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 12.3.51–52

A path for this age

The Vedic tradition describes four ages, each with its own most effective practice. As the ages decline, the method becomes simpler, and more merciful.

  1. Satya-yuga the age of truth

    Liberation through deep meditation.

  2. Tretā-yuga

    Through great sacrifices.

  3. Dvāpara-yuga

    Through temple worship.

  4. Kali-yuga our age

    Through hari-kīrtana, singing the names of God.

kaler doṣa-nidhe rājann asti hy eko mahān guṇaḥ / kīrtanād eva kṛṣṇasya mukta-saṅgaḥ paraṁ vrajet “O king, this age of Kali is an ocean of faults, yet it has one great quality. Simply by singing the name of [the all-attractive Personality of God], one becomes free and reaches the Supreme.” Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 12.3.51 (our rendering)

The best known form of this practice is the mahā-mantra, the “great chant,” found in the Kali-santaraṇa Upaniṣad:

हरे कृष्ण हरे कृष्ण कृष्ण कृष्ण हरे हरे । हरे राम हरे राम राम राम हरे हरे ॥ hare kṛṣṇa hare kṛṣṇa kṛṣṇa kṛṣṇa hare hare / hare rāma hare rāma rāma rāma hare hare It is often explained as a call to the Divine and to the Divine's own loving energy: “please engage me in Your service.”

Unity in diversity

Calling on the name, everywhere

The practice is not limited to one tradition. Wherever people have loved God, they have repeated God's name.

What the name does

Cleaning the mirror, putting out the fire

Śrī Caitanya, who spread congregational chanting through Bengal and Orissa in the early 1500s, summed up its effects in the first verse of his only written teaching:

ceto-darpaṇa-mārjanaṁ bhava-mahā-dāvāgni-nirvāpaṇam… “It cleans the mirror of the heart and puts out the great forest fire of worldly existence. It spreads the moonlight of good fortune, it is the life of wisdom, it swells the ocean of joy, it lets us taste full nectar at every step, and it bathes the whole self. May the singing of the name of [the all-attractive Personality of God] be victorious.” Śikṣāṣṭakam 1 (our rendering)

The themes on this site meet here. The name uncovers love that was covered but never lost. It burns karmic seeds. It weakens the guṇas and anarthas. And it reaches the deep imprints in the heart, through sound, attention and community, in a way anyone can join.

Practice

Two ways to chant

Together

Kīrtan

Singing the names aloud with others, usually with call and response, drums and hand cymbals. It is joyful, open to anyone, and needs no training. Singing together also settles the body and bonds a group, which is one reason it heals.

Alone

Japa

Softly repeating the name on a string of beads, a mālā. It is a daily meditation that steadies the mind and builds a private relationship with the Name. Prayer beads are found in Hindu, Buddhist, Christian and Islamic practice alike.

The right mood

The name is all-powerful, but how we chant matters. The bhakti tradition stresses attention and humility, and warns most of all against chanting while looking down on others. Śrī Caitanya gave the attitude in one verse:

tṛṇād api sunīcena taror api sahiṣṇunā / amāninā mānadena kīrtanīyaḥ sadā hariḥ “Humbler than a blade of grass, more tolerant than a tree, seeking no honour and giving honour to all: in this mood one should always sing the name of the Lord.” Śikṣāṣṭakam 3 (our rendering)
