Theme · What is karma?

The world as a school of love

Karma is often pictured as a ledger of punishments and rewards. We see it differently. Karma is the process by which this world brings out the best in us. Patiently and precisely, it teaches the soul to love without motive and without interruption.

The law of return

What we sow, we reap

Karma simply means “action.” Every action, word and thought sets something in motion, and what we set in motion comes back to us. This is one of the few teachings that nearly every tradition shares.

The Gītā adds a note of humility: gahanā karmaṇo gatiḥ, “the way of action is hard to understand” (4.17). We rarely see the whole chain of causes. That is why karma calls for trust more than calculation.

Three kinds of karma

Seeds, fruits and new planting

The Vedic tradition describes karma in three stages, like a farmer's year.

  • SañcitaThe store of seeds: everything we have set in motion over many lives, not yet ripe.
  • PrārabdhaThe ripening fruit: the part of that store we meet in this life, as our circumstances, body and events.
  • KriyamāṇaNew planting: the choices we make now. This is the only part fully in our hands.

Not punishment, but education

Why the world pushes back

If love is what the soul is made of, then every wrong action has the same root. At some moment we placed something passing above what is eternal. Karma returns that choice to us so that we can see it and choose again.

Seen this way, a loss, a betrayal or a failure is not the universe's revenge. It is a teacher, often a strict one, that loosens our grip on what cannot last and turns us back toward love. The traditions say the same in their own words.

How karma teaches love

Without motive, without interruption

The Bhāgavatam (1.2.6) names two marks of the highest love: ahaitukī, without motive, and apratihatā, without interruption. Karma trains us in both.

  1. Acting without grasping ahaitukī

    We act, and the results rarely match our plans. Slowly we learn to do what is right because it is right, and to let go of the outcome. The Gītā calls this karma-yoga.

  2. Loving through difficulty apratihatā

    Then life tests whether our love survives disappointment. When someone hurts us, does love stop? Each hard moment is a chance to keep loving anyway.

  3. Seeing mercy in everything bhakti

    At last we stop asking “why is this happening to me?” and start asking “what is love asking of me now?” Karma has done its work.

कर्मण्येवाधिकारस्ते मा फलेषु कदाचन karmaṇy evādhikāras te mā phaleṣu kadācana “Your right is to the action alone, never to its fruits.” Bhagavad-gītā 2.47 (our rendering)
tat te ’nukampāṁ su-samīkṣamāṇo bhuñjāna evātma-kṛtaṁ vipākam “One who looks for Your mercy in everything, while living through the results of their own past actions, and who offers You heart, words and body, inherits liberation as a child inherits from a parent.” Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.14.8 (our rendering)

Beyond karma

Love burns the seeds

Suffering can wear karma down slowly. Love does it at the root. When the heart turns fully toward God, the stored seeds lose their power to sprout.

karmāṇi nirdahati kintu ca bhakti-bhājām “He burns up the karma of those who are devoted to Him.” Brahma-saṁhitā 5.54 (our rendering)

How this happens, and how bhakti reaches the deepest impressions in the heart, is the subject of a later theme.
