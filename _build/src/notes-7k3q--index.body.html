Balance in bhakti · Study notes

Named instances: study notes

This page names specific groups, lineages and philosophies, and the risk of imbalance each one carries when measured against guru, sādhu and śāstra. It is a companion to the public directory, which describes the same tendencies without names.

Unlisted study notes, not linked from the site. They give the perspective of the Gauḍīya bhakti tradition, which coined several of these categories, and are not judgments of any individual. Many of the communities named are living traditions with their own history and their own self-understanding, and members today may not hold the views described. We record a risk, not a verdict.

Instance 1 · the thirteen apa-sampradāyas

Thirteen “unauthorised lines”

Gauḍīya teachers of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, notably in the circle of Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura and Bhaktisiddhānta Sarasvatī, warned against thirteen groups that claimed to follow Śrī Caitanya but, in their view, had left the guidance of guru, sādhu and śāstra. They are listed in a traditional Bengali verse whose original author is uncertain:

āula, bāula, kartābhajā, neḍā, daraveśa, sāṅi / sahajiyā, sakhībhekī, smārta, jāta-gosāñi / ativāḍī, cūḍādhārī, gaurāṅga-nāgarī Traditional verse of the Gauḍīya line; source uncertain

The core text they are measured against is the warning quoted by Rūpa Gosvāmī: “Devotion to Hari that ignores the śruti, smṛti, Purāṇas and Pañcarātra only causes disturbance” (Bhakti-rasāmṛta-sindhu 1.2.101, quoting the Brahma-yāmala; our rendering).

Instance 2 · Māyāvāda

Impersonal monism: disregard for the externals of bhakti

Who. The Kevala-advaita school associated with Śaṅkarācārya (8th c.) and its monastic orders (the Daśanāmī sannyāsīs and the maṭhas of Śṛṅgeri, Dvārakā, Purī and Jyotir), as later systematised; and its modern heirs: Neo-Vedānta, which treats all forms of God as equal symbols of one formless Absolute, and contemporary Neo-Advaita, which teaches that one is already enlightened and needs no practice.

What we honour. Śaṅkara restored the authority of the Vedas in his time, taught strict renunciation and the clear distinction between self and body, and himself wrote hymns of devotion: “Worship Govinda, worship Govinda, worship Govinda, O fool” (Bhaja Govindam, attributed to Śaṅkara).

Risk of imbalance. In this view the personal form of God (saguṇa brahman), the deity, the holy name, the pastimes and service are products of illusion (upādhi), useful only until the formless is realised. So the externals of bhakti are held loosely and finally dropped. This is jñāna-miśra devotion, and the “hollow renunciation” of Bhakti-rasāmṛta-sindhu 1.2.256. The Gauḍīya tradition also points to the soul being declared identical to God, which removes the relationship that bhakti is.

Śāstra. Gītā 7.24 (the unintelligent think the Lord's form is a temporary manifestation), 12.5 (the path to the unmanifest is more troublesome), 15.7 (the soul is an eternal part); Bhāgavatam 10.2.32 (those who think themselves liberated fall for neglecting the Lord's feet). Gauḍīya texts also cite the Padma Purāṇa's “māyāvādam asac-chāstraṁ pracchannaṁ bauddham ucyate” (Māyāvāda is called covered Buddhism); we note it as part of the tradition's self-understanding but do not use it on the public site, as it reads as polemic.

Public entries. “The form is only a step”; “I am already That”.

Instance 3 · Śākta worship

Worship of the Goddess for worldly ends, outside scripture

Who. Śākta traditions that worship the Devī (Durgā, Kālī, Tripurasundarī and others) as the supreme reality, especially where worship aims at material boons; and the left-hand tantric lines (vāmācāra, Kaula practice) that use the “five Ms” (pañca-makāra: wine, meat, fish, parched grain and ritual union) and animal sacrifice.

What we honour. The Goddess is the Lord's own energy: “Durgā, the power of creation, maintenance and dissolution, moves like His shadow and acts according to His will. I worship Govinda, the original Person” (Brahma-saṁhitā 5.44, our rendering). The gopīs themselves worshipped the goddess Kātyāyanī, praying for Kṛṣṇa as their husband (Bhāgavatam 10.22.4). Worship of the Devī in service of the Lord is a beautiful part of the tradition.

Risk of imbalance. Two drifts. First, disregard of śāstra: rites that the Vedic scriptures restrict or place in the mode of darkness (Gītā 17.4) are treated as the path. Second, materialistic pursuit: the Goddess is approached for wealth, power, victory or the harming of enemies, which is karma-miśra devotion at best (Gītā 7.20, those whose knowledge is stolen by desires turn to other gods; 7.23, their fruit is temporary). The Gītā is gentle even here: those who worship other gods with faith worship the Lord too, “but not in the right way” (9.23).

Public entries. “Worship for what it brings”; “Practice outside scripture”.

Instance 4 · vikarma

The capitalist ugra-karma mentality

Who. Not a sect but a mentality, and today the dominant one: growth at any cost, profit as the measure of value, and an economy built on exploiting the earth, animals and workers. It shows up in industrial animal slaughter, extractive industry that poisons land and water, debt and speculation, and a consumer culture that manufactures desire. Religious groups are not immune: the “prosperity gospel”, and spiritual movements that sell practice as a path to wealth, bring the same mentality into worship.

What we honour. Honest work, trade and wealth are part of dharma. The Gītā gives the vaiśya the duty of agriculture, cow protection and trade (18.44), and wealth used in God's service and for others' welfare is blessed. The problem is not wealth; it is greed made into a system.

Risk of imbalance. Work done outside the order of nature and scripture is vikarma (Gītā 4.17), and it binds. The Gītā describes the mentality exactly: “Today I gained this, tomorrow I will fulfil that desire. This is mine, and that wealth will be mine too” (16.13); such people, lost to themselves, “engage in fierce works (ugra-karma) for the destruction of the world” (16.9). One who enjoys without offering is “a thief” (3.12). This is karma without even the miśra, and it shapes the karma of whole societies, not just individuals (see What is karma?).

Śāstra. Īśopaniṣad 1 (everything belongs to the Lord; take only your share, do not covet); Gītā 3.12, 4.17, 16.9, 16.13–15; Bhāgavatam 7.14.8 (one may claim only what is needed to maintain the body; one who takes more is a thief and deserves punishment).

Public entry. “Endless accumulation”.

Instance 5 · Ramakrishna Mission

Ramakrishna Mission and Swami Vivekananda

Who. The Ramakrishna Math and Mission, founded in 1897 by Swami Vivekananda in the name of his teacher Śrī Rāmakṛṣṇa Paramahaṁsa, and the wider Neo-Vedānta it popularised in India and the West (see also instance 2).

What we honour. Rāmakṛṣṇa’s intense, childlike devotion to the Divine Mother and his openness to every faith; Vivekananda’s courage in bringing Vedānta to the West, his call to strength and character, and the Mission’s real, large-scale relief work, schools and hospitals. The Mission’s motto, ātmano mokṣārthaṁ jagad-hitāya ca, “for one’s own liberation and for the good of the world”, joins the inner and the outer life.

Risk of imbalance. Three points nik names. (a) Meat and beef eating. Vivekananda spoke in favour of meat eating to build strength in the conditions of his time, and in some reported conversations did not exclude beef; the Mission does not ask its members to be vegetarian. In śāstra, food is offered to the Lord before it is eaten and ahiṁsā is part of sattva; cow protection is named as a natural duty (Gītā 18.44). Eating for one’s own sake is called eating sin (Gītā 3.13). (b) “Serving people is serving God” (śiva-jñāne jīva-sevā; Vivekananda’s daridra-nārāyaṇa, “God in the poor”). The compassion is beautiful; the risk is that service to the body and society replaces service to God, and the poor are called God while God Himself is left out. Śāstra places the order the other way round: water the root and every branch is fed. (c) “Yata mata, tata patha” (“as many faiths, so many paths”, Rāmakṛṣṇa). This is close to our own thesis of unity in diversity, and we honour it. The risk is the next step: “so all paths lead to the same place, and the choice of path, deity or practice does not matter”. The Gītā says each path reaches its own goal, and the Lord reciprocates with each as they approach. We hold every path as a stepping stone, not every stone as the summit. The underlying philosophy is Neo-Vedānta, with the jñāna-miśra risk of instance 2.

Śāstra. Gītā 3.13, 9.26 (a leaf, a flower, fruit or water offered with love), 18.44; Bhāgavatam 4.31.14 (as watering the root of a tree nourishes the trunk, branches and leaves, so worship of Acyuta satisfies all); Gītā 4.11 (as they approach Me, so I reciprocate), 9.25 (worshippers of the devas go to the devas, My devotees come to Me).

Public entries. “All paths are the same”; “Service without the root”; “Violence on the plate”; “The form is only a step”.

Instance 6 · Pūrva-mīmāṁsā

Ritualism without God

Who. The classical Pūrva-mīmāṁsā school (Jaimini, Kumārila Bhaṭṭa, Prabhākara), which holds the Vedic rites to be self-sufficient: the ritual itself produces the result, and a supreme God is not needed. Its spirit lives on wherever religion is reduced to correct procedure for merit.

What we honour. Its rigour in reading the Veda and its insistence on duty and discipline. Much of later Vedānta, including Vaiṣṇava Vedānta, uses its rules of interpretation.

Risk of imbalance. The textbook case of karma-miśra, and even karma without bhakti. The Gītā describes those “charmed by the flowery words of the Veda”, who say there is nothing beyond them and seek heaven, enjoyment and power (2.42–44).

Śāstra. Gītā 2.42–46, 9.20–21; Bhāgavatam 1.2.8 (dharma that does not awaken attraction to hearing about the Lord is only wasted labour).

Public entries. “Rules above love”; “Worship for what it brings”.

Instance 7 · siddhi- and energy-centred yoga

Yoga for powers, energy or fitness

Who. Classical haṭha and tantric kuṇḍalinī lines aimed at mystic powers (siddhis) or energy experiences, and modern postural yoga taught only as fitness, wellness or stress relief.

What we honour. Real discipline of body, breath and mind; the Gītā itself teaches a yoga of posture and meditation (6.10–17), and many people first meet the spiritual life through a yoga class.

Risk of imbalance. Yoga-miśra: the means becomes the goal. Powers and experiences feed the ego, and yoga cut off from the Lord stays a technique. Kṛṣṇa calls the siddhis obstacles and a waste of time for one who seeks the highest yoga.

Śāstra. Bhāgavatam 11.15.33; Gītā 6.47; Yoga-sūtra 3.37, 3.38 in some editions (the powers are obstacles to samādhi, though perfections to the outward mind).

Public entries. “Power and technique”.

Instance 8 · sectarian exclusivism

“Only our way”

Who. Any group, including Vaiṣṇava ones, that treats its own institution, founder or lineage as the only valid path, and shows contempt for sincere devotees and seekers outside it.

What we honour. Loyalty to one’s own teacher and tradition is a virtue, and conviction about one’s path gives strength (niścaya, Upadeśāmṛta 3).

Risk of imbalance. Loyalty hardens into pride. It leads straight to the offence the tradition fears most, contempt for devotees (vaiṣṇava-aparādha, the “mad elephant”), and it contradicts this movement’s own thesis of unity in diversity.

Śāstra. Caitanya-caritāmṛta, Madhya 19.156; Śikṣāṣṭaka 3 (humbler than a blade of grass, more tolerant than a tree, giving respect to all); Bhāgavatam 11.28.1.

Public entries. “Only our way”.

Instance 9 · guru-ism in modern institutions

The founder above the teaching

Who. Modern movements of any tradition organised around a single charismatic founder or living master, where the teacher’s word is final and is not checked against scripture or the wider community of saints. The Kartābhajā risk (instance 1) in modern form.

What we honour. The guru is essential in every bhakti line, and deep gratitude and obedience to a genuine teacher are a mark of a sincere student.

Risk of imbalance. Without śāstra and sādhu, the guru is unchecked, and abuses of power, money and trust follow. Students lose the ability to discern.

Śāstra. Narottama’s sādhu-śāstra-guru-vākya; Gītā 4.2 and 4.34 (knowledge received in succession, from those who have seen the truth); Caitanya-caritāmṛta, Madhya 8.128.

Public entries. “The teacher above the teaching”.

Instance 10 · premature rāgānugā

Meditating on the summit too early

Who. Within Gauḍīya circles: taking up meditation on the intimate pastimes of Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa and on one’s own eternal spiritual identity before the heart is purified of lust and pride. The Sahajiyā risk (instance 1) in a milder, private form.

What we honour. Rāgānugā bhakti, spontaneous devotion following the residents of Vraja, is the very goal Rūpa Gosvāmī describes, and genuine greed for it is a great fortune.

Risk of imbalance. Imitation instead of attainment: sensual imagination mistaken for spiritual vision, pride in being “advanced”, and neglect of the basic practices of hearing, chanting and humility.

Śāstra. Bhakti-rasāmṛta-sindhu 1.4.15–16 (the stages from faith to love); Bhāgavatam 10.33.30–31 (one who is not a controller should never imitate the Lord’s rāsa, even in the mind).

Public entries. “Imitating the summit”.

Instance 11 · “law of attraction” spirituality

Manifesting wealth through thought

Who. The New Thought and “law of attraction” current (popular through books and courses such as The Secret), which teaches that focused thought and feeling attract wealth, health and success.

What we honour. The insight that our inner state shapes our outer life, which agrees with what this site teaches about karma and imprints (see What is karma?).

Risk of imbalance. Karma-miśra in modern dress: the universe, or God, becomes a supplier of wishes, and spirituality is measured by success. It strengthens the very desires that bind. Lazarev’s view is the reverse: love that does not depend on outcomes is what protects.

Śāstra. Gītā 7.20–23, 16.13–15; Īśopaniṣad 1.

Public entries. “Worship for what it brings”; “Endless accumulation”.

Instance 12 · tantra of the five Ms

Left-hand tantra and the pañca-makāra

Who. Left-hand (vāmācāra) and Kaula tantric lines that use the “five Ms” (pañca-makāra): madya (wine), māṁsa (meat), matsya (fish), mudrā (parched grain, or in some readings gesture) and maithuna (ritual sexual union) as a means of transcendence; and the modern Western “neo-tantra” of sacred sexuality workshops, which keeps the fifth M and drops the rest. Closely linked with instance 3.

What we honour. “Tantra” is a wide word. The Vaiṣṇava Pañcarātra āgamas, which govern temple worship, mantra and initiation in the bhakti traditions, are themselves tantras, and right-hand (dakṣiṇa) tantra is a disciplined path of worship. Even the five-M schools often teach that they are meant for a very few, under strict guidance, and some read the five Ms symbolically.

Risk of imbalance. Indulgence is sanctified instead of regulated. The very things the Veda allows only under restraint, as a way to gradually give them up, become the path itself. The result is the mode of darkness called spirituality, with intoxication and sexuality bound up with power over others. This is the clearest case of “practice outside scripture” and of lust dressed as love.

Śāstra. Bhāgavatam 11.5.11 (sex, meat and wine are natural tendencies that scripture does not command; it regulates them through marriage, sacrifice and the ritual cup, and the aim is to give them up); Gītā 16.23, 17.4–10; Bhakti-rasāmṛta-sindhu 1.2.101.

Public entries. “Practice outside scripture”; “Sense pleasure called spirituality”.

Instance 13 · Buddhism

Morality on the mental, not the spiritual, platform

Who. The Buddhist traditions (Theravāda, Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna), measured here only from the Vaiṣṇava point of view, as a teaching that works on the level of mind and ethics. Buddhism has its own rich self-understanding, and many Buddhists would describe their path differently.

What we honour. The Vaiṣṇava tradition counts the Buddha among the avatāras (Bhāgavatam 1.3.24). Jayadeva sings that, out of compassion, the Buddha set aside the Vedic rules of sacrifice to stop the killing of animals: “keśava dhṛta-buddha-śarīra jaya jagad-īśa hare” (Gīta-govinda 1, Daśāvatāra-stotra). Buddhism’s ethics, mindfulness, non-violence and compassion (mettā, karuṇā) are a model for any seeker, and its analysis of the mind is the clearest map of the first of our four levels of awareness. Pure Land devotion to Amitābha shows the bhakti impulse within Buddhism itself.

Risk of imbalance. Goodness of mind is taken for the goal. Ethics and meditation purify the mind, but the teaching of no-self (anattā) and of nirvāṇa as the extinguishing of craving and selfhood stops before the eternal soul and the personal God. Compassion without a final Object of love, and peace as cessation rather than fullness, keep the seeker on the platform of sattva, which still binds by attachment to happiness and knowledge (Gītā 14.6). Seen from bhakti, this is a noble jñāna path that stops one step short.

Śāstra. Gītā 2.20 (the soul is never born and never dies), 14.6, 14.26 (one who serves Me with unfailing devotion rises above the three guṇas); Bhāgavatam 1.3.24.

Public entries. “Goodness without the soul”.
