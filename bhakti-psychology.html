Themes · Bhakti and modern psychology

Healing at the root

Modern psychology has found that our past lives on in us as patterns, in the mind and in the body. The Vedic sages called these patterns saṁskāras and vāsanās, and described a deeper layer still, in the subtle body. Bhakti, we believe, reaches that deepest layer, because love works where effort alone cannot.

This page offers a spiritual perspective. If you live with trauma, depression or anxiety, bhakti works best alongside good professional care, never instead of it.

Two vocabularies

One map of the inner world

Psychology and the Vedic tradition describe the same territory from different sides. The correspondences are not exact, but they are close enough to let each shed light on the other.

Vedic termMeaningModern parallel
SaṁskāraimpressionThe trace left by every experience and actionMemory traces, implicit memory, conditioning
VāsanātendencyA cluster of impressions that pushes us to act and react in set waysSchemas, habits, Jung's “complexes”
KleśaafflictionThe root distortions: ignorance, ego, craving, aversion, fearCore beliefs, approach and avoidance drives, threat responses
Anartha“without value”Unwanted patterns that cover loveMaladaptive patterns, defences, compulsions
Hṛdaya-granthiknot of the heartThe deepest knot of false identity and desireCore wound, unresolved trauma
SākṣīwitnessAwareness that observes without being caughtThe observing self, mindful “decentering”

Trauma as vāsanā

An imprint on three levels

In our understanding, trauma is a deep vāsanā: an imprint pressed so hard into the self that it keeps replaying long after the event is over. It is stored on three levels at once.

  • The brainAs memories and alarm patterns. Neuroscience shows that pathways used often grow stronger, and that a memory, when recalled, can be updated.
  • The bodyAs tension, reflexes and nervous-system responses. Bessel van der Kolk's widely read book on trauma is titled, fittingly, The Body Keeps the Score.
  • The subtle bodyAs vāsanās that the Vedic tradition says can carry from life to life. Some fears and attractions seem to have no cause in this lifetime.

This is why talking alone sometimes is not enough, and why the body and the spirit both have a part to play in healing.

The cycle · Yoga Sūtras

How an imprint becomes a destiny

The yoga tradition describes a cycle that turns until something interrupts it:

  1. Saṁskāra

    An experience leaves an impression.

  2. Vāsanā

    Impressions gather into a tendency.

  3. Vṛtti

    The tendency rises as thoughts and feelings.

  4. Karma

    We act on them, and the action returns as circumstances.

  5. New saṁskāra

    The circumstances leave new impressions, and the wheel turns again.

Patañjali names the five roots that keep it turning (Yoga Sūtras 2.3): avidyā, not knowing who we are; asmitā, identifying with body and mind; rāga, craving; dveṣa, aversion; and abhiniveśa, clinging to life out of fear. Trauma lives mostly in the last two. It is aversion and fear that never got to finish.

He also says how they are removed (2.10–2.11). Their active forms are calmed by meditation. Their subtle seeds are dissolved only by tracing them back to their source. This is where bhakti comes in.

Bhakti at the root

Burning the seeds before they sprout

Therapy and meditation can calm the branches of a pattern. The bhakti tradition claims something more: that devotion reaches even the seeds that have not yet sprouted.

aprārabdha-phalaṁ pāpaṁ kūṭaṁ bījaṁ phalonmukham / krameṇaiva pralīyeta viṣṇu-bhakti-ratātmanām “Reactions not yet begun, those lying dormant, those still in seed form and those about to bear fruit: all of them gradually dissolve for those absorbed in devotion to the Lord.” Padma Purāṇa, quoted in Bhakti-rasāmṛta-sindhu 1.1.18 (our rendering)

The Bhāgavatam (1.2.17–21) describes the process step by step:

  1. Hearing

    As we hear about the Divine, the Lord in the heart, the friend of the sincere, begins washing away what is harmful there (1.2.17).

  2. Steadiness

    When the harmful is almost gone, devotion becomes steady (1.2.18).

  3. Freedom from passion and inertia

    Lust, greed and dullness lose their grip, and the mind settles in goodness and joy (1.2.19).

  4. The knot is cut

    “The knot of the heart is pierced, all doubts are cut to pieces, and the chain of karma ends” (1.2.21, our rendering).

Why love heals

What psychology can see in bhakti

Psychology cannot measure grace. But it can see why the practices of devotion help heal the heart.

  • A secure baseAttachment research shows that we heal in safe relationships. Psychologists of religion, such as Lee Kirkpatrick, have studied how a loving relationship with God can work as that secure base.
  • The witnessPrayer and chanting train us to observe our thoughts instead of becoming them, much like mindfulness-based therapies do.
  • A higher tastePatterns are hard to suppress but easier to outgrow. The Gītā (2.59) says even the taste for lower things fades when a higher taste is found.
  • ForgivenessLetting go of resentment is one of the most studied paths to emotional recovery. Bhakti makes it natural, because we see others as souls who belong to God.
  • Sound and communitySinging together calms the body and bonds people. That is part of the power of kīrtan, the subject of our next theme.
  • MeaningSuffering becomes bearable when it has a purpose. Bhakti gives every experience one: to love more.

Peeling back the layers

Forgiveness goes deeper than we think

Lazarev describes inner healing as peeling back layers. On top lies resentment against ourselves and our fate. Beneath it lie contempt, hatred and condemnation of others. And at the root lies placing our attachment to earthly things above our love for God (Diagnostics of Karma, Book 2, p. 113 of the Russian original). Healing the top layer without reaching the root brings only temporary relief.

This is the work of bhakti: turning the heart back toward love, so that the oldest knots can finally loosen.
