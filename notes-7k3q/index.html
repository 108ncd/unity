Balance in bhakti · Study notes

Named instances: study notes

This page names specific groups, lineages and philosophies, and the risk of imbalance each one carries when measured against guru, sādhu and śāstra. It is a companion to the public directory, which describes the same tendencies without names.

Unlisted study notes, not linked from the site. They give the perspective of the Gauḍīya bhakti tradition, which coined several of these categories, and are not judgments of any individual. Many of the communities named are living traditions with their own history and their own self-understanding, and members today may not hold the views described. We record a risk, not a verdict.

Instance 1 · the thirteen apa-sampradāyas

Thirteen “unauthorised lines”

Gauḍīya teachers of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, notably in the circle of Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura and Bhaktisiddhānta Sarasvatī, warned against thirteen groups that claimed to follow Śrī Caitanya but, in their view, had left the guidance of guru, sādhu and śāstra. They are listed in a traditional Bengali verse whose original author is uncertain:

āula, bāula, kartābhajā, neḍā, daraveśa, sāṅi / sahajiyā, sakhībhekī, smārta, jāta-gosāñi / ativāḍī, cūḍādhārī, gaurāṅga-nāgarī Traditional verse of the Gauḍīya line; source uncertain

The core text they are measured against is the warning quoted by Rūpa Gosvāmī: “Devotion to Hari that ignores the śruti, smṛti, Purāṇas and Pañcarātra only causes disturbance” (Bhakti-rasāmṛta-sindhu 1.2.101, quoting the Brahma-yāmala; our rendering).

The test the tradition gives is the Padma Purāṇa verse that names, among grave errors, seeing the deity as stone, the guru as an ordinary person and a Vaiṣṇava in terms of birth (vaiṣṇave jāti-buddhiḥ); and Caitanya-caritāmṛta, Madhya 8.128: whoever knows the truth of Kṛṣṇa is guru.

Instance 2 · Māyāvāda

Impersonal monism: disregard for the externals of bhakti

Who. The Kevala-advaita school associated with Śaṅkarācārya (8th c.) and its monastic orders (the Daśanāmī sannyāsīs and the maṭhas of Śṛṅgeri, Dvārakā, Purī and Jyotir), as later systematised; and its modern heirs: Neo-Vedānta, which treats all forms of God as equal symbols of one formless Absolute, and contemporary Neo-Advaita, which teaches that one is already enlightened and needs no practice.

What we honour. Śaṅkara restored the authority of the Vedas in his time, taught strict renunciation and the clear distinction between self and body, and himself wrote hymns of devotion: “Worship Govinda, worship Govinda, worship Govinda, O fool” (Bhaja Govindam, attributed to Śaṅkara).

Risk of imbalance. In this view the personal form of God (saguṇa brahman), the deity, the holy name, the pastimes and service are products of illusion (upādhi), useful only until the formless is realised. So the externals of bhakti are held loosely and finally dropped. This is jñāna-miśra devotion, and the “hollow renunciation” of Bhakti-rasāmṛta-sindhu 1.2.256. The Gauḍīya tradition also points to the soul being declared identical to God, which removes the relationship that bhakti is.

Śāstra. Gītā 7.24 (the unintelligent think the Lord's form is a temporary manifestation), 12.5 (the path to the unmanifest is more troublesome), 15.7 (the soul is an eternal part); Bhāgavatam 10.2.32 (those who think themselves liberated fall for neglecting the Lord's feet). Gauḍīya texts also cite the Padma Purāṇa's “māyāvādam asac-chāstraṁ pracchannaṁ bauddham ucyate” (Māyāvāda is called covered Buddhism); we note it as part of the tradition's self-understanding but do not use it on the public site, as it reads as polemic.

Public entries. “The form is only a step”; “I am already That”.

Instance 3 · Śākta worship

Worship of the Goddess for worldly ends, outside scripture

Who. Śākta traditions that worship the Devī (Durgā, Kālī, Tripurasundarī and others) as the supreme reality, especially where worship aims at material boons; and the left-hand tantric lines (vāmācāra, Kaula practice) that use the “five Ms” (pañca-makāra: wine, meat, fish, parched grain and ritual union) and animal sacrifice.

What we honour. The Goddess is the Lord's own energy: “Durgā, the power of creation, maintenance and dissolution, moves like His shadow and acts according to His will. I worship Govinda, the original Person” (Brahma-saṁhitā 5.44, our rendering). The gopīs themselves worshipped the goddess Kātyāyanī, praying for Kṛṣṇa as their husband (Bhāgavatam 10.22.4). Worship of the Devī in service of the Lord is a beautiful part of the tradition.

Risk of imbalance. Two drifts. First, disregard of śāstra: rites that the Vedic scriptures restrict or place in the mode of darkness (Gītā 17.4) are treated as the path. Second, materialistic pursuit: the Goddess is approached for wealth, power, victory or the harming of enemies, which is karma-miśra devotion at best (Gītā 7.20, those whose knowledge is stolen by desires turn to other gods; 7.23, their fruit is temporary). The Gītā is gentle even here: those who worship other gods with faith worship the Lord too, “but not in the right way” (9.23).

Public entries. “Worship for what it brings”; “Practice outside scripture”.

Instance 4 · vikarma

The capitalist ugra-karma mentality

Who. Not a sect but a mentality, and today the dominant one: growth at any cost, profit as the measure of value, and an economy built on exploiting the earth, animals and workers. It shows up in industrial animal slaughter, extractive industry that poisons land and water, debt and speculation, and a consumer culture that manufactures desire. Religious groups are not immune: the “prosperity gospel”, and spiritual movements that sell practice as a path to wealth, bring the same mentality into worship.

What we honour. Honest work, trade and wealth are part of dharma. The Gītā gives the vaiśya the duty of agriculture, cow protection and trade (18.44), and wealth used in God's service and for others' welfare is blessed. The problem is not wealth; it is greed made into a system.

Risk of imbalance. Work done outside the order of nature and scripture is vikarma (Gītā 4.17), and it binds. The Gītā describes the mentality exactly: “Today I gained this, tomorrow I will fulfil that desire. This is mine, and that wealth will be mine too” (16.13); such people, lost to themselves, “engage in fierce works (ugra-karma) for the destruction of the world” (16.9). One who enjoys without offering is “a thief” (3.12). This is karma without even the miśra, and it shapes the karma of whole societies, not just individuals (see What is karma?).

Śāstra. Īśopaniṣad 1 (everything belongs to the Lord; take only your share, do not covet); Gītā 3.12, 4.17, 16.9, 16.13–15; Bhāgavatam 7.14.8 (one may claim only what is needed to maintain the body; one who takes more is a thief and deserves punishment).

Public entry. “Endless accumulation”.

Candidates for further instances

Suggestions for the directory, for nik to accept, change or drop.

  • Pūrva-mīmāṁsā ritualism: the classical school that holds Vedic ritual to be self-sufficient, with God unnecessary. The textbook case of karma-miśra (Gītā 2.42–44).
  • Siddhi- and energy-centred yoga: classical haṭha and kuṇḍalinī lines aimed at powers, and modern postural yoga as fitness or wellness only. Yoga-miśra (Bhāgavatam 11.15.33).
  • Sectarian exclusivism: any group, including Vaiṣṇava ones, that treats its own institution as the only path and shows contempt for other sincere devotees and traditions. The risk of Vaiṣṇava-aparādha, and directly against this movement's thesis.
  • Guru-ism in modern institutions: movements organised around a single charismatic founder without the check of scripture and the wider community of saints (the Kartābhajā risk in modern form).
  • Premature rāgānugā: within Gauḍīya circles, taking up meditation on the intimate pastimes and one's spiritual identity before the heart is purified (the Sahajiyā risk in a milder form).
  • “Law of attraction” spirituality: manifesting wealth and success through thought. A New Age form of karma-miśra.
