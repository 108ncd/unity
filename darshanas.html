Ṣaḍ-darśana · the six ways of seeing

Six schools, every question philosophy asks

Darśana means “seeing.” The six classical schools of Vedic thought each look at reality from one angle. Together they map the ground that modern philosophy still walks.

Traditionally the six come in three pairs. Each pair joins a theory to a practice or a method to a conclusion: Nyāya and Vaiśeṣika (how we know and what exists), Sāṅkhya and Yoga (what the self is and how to realise it), Mīmāṁsā and Vedānta (how to live by the Veda and what the Veda finally teaches).

These are correspondences, not claims of direct influence. Most modern thinkers worked without knowledge of the darśanas. The parallels show that the Vedic schools had already mapped the same questions, often in great depth.

Vedānta's many voices

One sūtra, many readings

Vedānta grew into several schools that read the Brahma Sūtras differently. Between them they cover nearly every position modern philosophy of religion takes on how the self and God relate.

Advaita

Śaṅkara

Non-dualism: the self is Brahman, and difference is ultimately unreal. Echoed in absolute idealism.

Viśiṣṭādvaita

Rāmānuja

Qualified non-dualism: souls and world are real, as the body of God. Echoed in panentheism.

Dvaita

Madhva

Dualism: God, souls and matter are eternally distinct. Echoed in classical theism.

Dvaitādvaita

Nimbārka

Difference and non-difference held together, centred on Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa.

Śuddhādvaita

Vallabha

Pure non-dualism: the world is a real manifestation of Kṛṣṇa, approached through grace.

Acintya-bhedābheda

Caitanya, Baladeva Vidyābhūṣaṇa

Inconceivable oneness and difference: the soul is one with God in quality and distinct in quantity, and its nature is love.

Beyond the six

The other Indian schools

The darśanas grew up in conversation with schools that did not accept the Veda's authority. They belong on the same map. Cārvāka held that perception alone is valid and matter alone is real, anticipating modern materialism and empiricism. Buddhist thought explored impermanence, no-self and emptiness, which resonate with process philosophy and phenomenology. Jain anekāntavāda taught that truth has many sides and every view is partial, an early and generous form of perspectivism.

Where the inquiry leads

From seeing to loving

In our view the six schools form a single ascent. Nyāya sharpens how we know. Vaiśeṣika surveys what exists. Sāṅkhya separates the self from what is not the self. Yoga steadies the mind so the self can be seen. Mīmāṁsā orders life in duty. Vedānta asks the last question: to whom does the self belong?

The bhakti schools of Vedānta answer that the self belongs to God in love. The Gītā (18.55) says it in one line:

भक्त्या मामभिजानाति यावान्यश्चास्मि तत्त्वतः bhaktyā mām abhijānāti yāvān yaś cāsmi tattvataḥ “One can understand Me as I am, as the Supreme Personality of Godhead, only by devotional service.” Bhagavad-gītā 18.55, trans. Prabhupāda
