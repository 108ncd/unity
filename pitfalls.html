Theme · Pitfalls on the path

What dims love, and what makes it shine

Love for God is never lost, but it can be covered or uncovered every day. Some habits feed it and some starve it. Knowing which is which is half the path.

Bhakti-latā · the creeper of devotion

A living plant

The Caitanya-caritāmṛta (Madhya 19.151–160) pictures bhakti as a creeper that grows from a seed planted in the heart. Watered by hearing and remembering, it climbs higher and higher until it reaches the Divine. But weeds grow beside it, and they drink the same water. If they are not pulled out, they choke the creeper. And one careless step, like a mad elephant trampling a garden, can uproot it altogether: contempt for those who love God.

So the path has two sides. We water what is good, and we watch for what grows alongside it.

Upadeśāmṛta 2 · Rūpa Gosvāmī

Six things that weaken devotion

Rūpa Gosvāmī, a sixteenth-century teacher of bhakti, named six habits that slowly drain love. They apply to any sincere seeker.

  • AtyāhāraTaking more than we need: food, money, possessions, attention.
  • PrayāsaExhausting ourselves for things that will not last.
  • PrajalpaIdle talk and gossip, which scatter the mind.
  • NiyamāgrahaBeing too rigid with rules, or too careless about them.
  • Jana-saṅgaKeeping company that pulls the heart away from God.
  • LaulyamRestless craving, and jumping from one thing to the next.

Upadeśāmṛta 3

Six things that nourish it

In the very next verse he gives the remedy: six qualities that help love grow.

  • UtsāhaEnthusiasm: practising with a willing heart.
  • NiścayaConfidence that the path leads where it promises.
  • DhairyaPatience when progress feels slow.
  • Tat-tat-karma-pravartanaSteady practice, done in the right spirit.
  • Saṅga-tyāgaStepping back from what drags us down.
  • Sato vṛttiFollowing the example of the saintly.

Anarthas · the weeds within

What the heart carries

The most dangerous pitfalls are not outside us. They are the imprints we carry inside: old desires, resentments, fears and habits. The tradition calls them anarthas, “things without value.” They shape our consciousness, our consciousness shapes our actions, and our actions return to us as karma. A person can pray every day and still be held back by a grudge they never let go of.

Lazarev points to the same inner weeds. In his view the greatest danger to the soul is to let love die through resentment, contempt and condemnation of others.

That is why the four levels of awareness matter here. The more clearly we see that we are not our patterns, the less power they have to pull us away from love. See Four levels of awareness.

The three guṇas · Bhagavad-gītā 14

The three forces that colour the mind

The Gītā teaches that everything in material nature, including our own mind, is woven from three guṇas, literally “ropes.” They are sattva (goodness, clarity), rajas (passion, restlessness) and tamas (ignorance, inertia). At every moment one of them is strongest in us, and it colours how we see, what we want and how we act.

सत्त्वं रजस्तम इति गुणाः प्रकृतिसम्भवाः sattvaṁ rajas tama iti guṇāḥ prakṛti-sambhavāḥ / nibadhnanti mahā-bāho dehe dehinam avyayam “Goodness, passion and ignorance are the qualities born of nature. They bind the eternal soul to the body.” Bhagavad-gītā 14.5 (our rendering)

The guṇas also explain the two ladders below. The Gītā (14.18) says that those in goodness rise, those in passion stay in the middle, and those in ignorance sink. Our food, our company, our use of time and the places we spend it all carry one of the three colours, which is why the Gītā (chapter 17) looks at each of them through this lens.

The Buddhist tradition names a similar trio of inner forces, the three poisons: greed, hatred and delusion. The two maps are not identical, but they point at the same weeds.

Beyond the three

Even goodness is still a rope. Bhakti does not just move us from tamas to sattva. It lifts us beyond all three, into what the tradition calls śuddha-sattva, pure goodness, where love is no longer conditioned by moods.

māṁ ca yo ’vyabhicāreṇa bhakti-yogena sevate / sa guṇān samatītyaitān brahma-bhūyāya kalpate “One who serves Me with unwavering devotion rises above these guṇas and becomes fit to realise Brahman.” Bhagavad-gītā 14.26 (our rendering)

Two directions

How we fall, and how we rise

Both descent and ascent happen in steps. Neither is sudden, even when it feels that way.

Degradation · Bhagavad-gītā 2.62–63

The way down

  1. Dwelling on the objects of the senses

  2. Attachment

  3. Desire

  4. Anger, when desire is blocked

  5. Delusion

  6. Forgetting who we are

  7. Loss of discernment, and the fall

Elevation · Bhakti-rasāmṛta-sindhu 1.4.15–16

The way up

  1. Śraddhā: faith

  2. Sādhu-saṅga: good company

  3. Bhajana-kriyā: regular practice

  4. Anartha-nivṛtti: the weeds fall away

  5. Niṣṭhā: steadiness

  6. Ruci: taste

  7. Āsakti: attachment to God

  8. Bhāva: the first glimpse of love

  9. Prema: pure love

The way down starts with where we put our attention, and it is driven by tamas and rajas. The way up starts there too, steadied by sattva and carried beyond it by bhakti. Every day we choose which ladder to stand on.

Consequences

No sincere effort is ever lost

Falling does not cancel what we have gained. The Gītā is clear that progress on the spiritual path is never wasted, even if we stumble or leave it for a time.

svalpam apy asya dharmasya trāyate mahato bhayāt “Even a little of this practice protects one from great fear.” Bhagavad-gītā 2.40 (our rendering)
na hi kalyāṇa-kṛt kaścid durgatiṁ tāta gacchati “One who does good, my friend, never comes to a bad end.” Bhagavad-gītā 6.40 (our rendering)

The consequences of neglect are real: the heart grows dry, old patterns return, and karma tightens again. But the door back is always open. Love is still there, waiting under the weeds.
